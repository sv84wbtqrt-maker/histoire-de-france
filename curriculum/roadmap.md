# Roadmap: the history of France, in order

Goal: a solid general culture of French history, from the first humans to today. The learner should
understand the big timeline, the causes and consequences of key events, and the people who shaped
them, and be able to tell the story in their own words.

## How the course works
- One lesson per day, copied by hand in English in 10 to 15 minutes (about 180 to 300 words to write).
- A new lesson only after the previous one is marked "written".
- **Chronological order.** Each lesson is a step in one continuous story: it starts where the previous
  one stopped, and ends with what comes next.
- **Every 7th lesson (7, 14, 21, ...) is a review lesson**: no new content; a mini timeline of the last
  six lessons, one "cause → consequence" chain, and questions.
- **Links**: every lesson links back to earlier ones with (→ L<n>), especially to explain long-term
  causes (e.g. the Revolution → the financial crisis of Louis XVI → the wars of Louis XIV).
- **Recurring threads** to follow across the centuries (mention them when they come back):
  1. The State: how a king's small domain became a centralised country.
  2. Religion and the Church: Christianisation, wars of religion, secularism (laïcité).
  3. The borders and the map of France.
  4. Paris and the regions.
  5. Rights and the people: who has power, who can vote.
  6. France and the world: wars, empire, Europe.
- **The learner's questions come first** (within 3 lessons). Topics below are a guide; the teacher can
  split, merge or add lessons. Numbers are estimates; reviews are added at every multiple of 7.
- **Facts**: dates and figures must be correct. When a date is debated (e.g. Clovis's baptism), say so
  ("around 496–508"). Use the usual English names (Charlemagne, Joan of Arc, Louis XIV), with the French
  name in brackets the first time when useful.

## Period 0: the frame
- The map of time: the main periods of French history, and how to count centuries

## Period 1: before France (to the 5th century)
- The first humans: Tautavel, Chauvet and Lascaux
- The Neolithic revolution and the megaliths of Carnac
- Greeks in Marseille (Massalia, around 600 BC) and the Celts
- The Gauls: society, druids, tribes
- Caesar's conquest of Gaul and Vercingetorix (Gergovia and Alesia, 52 BC)
- Roman Gaul: cities, roads, Latin, the Gallo-Romans
- Early Christianity in Gaul
- Crisis of the Roman Empire and the Germanic peoples; the end of the Western Empire (476)

## Period 2: the Franks and the early Middle Ages (5th to 10th century)
- Clovis: king of the Franks, baptism, Paris
- The Merovingians: division of the kingdom, "lazy kings"
- Charles Martel and the battle of Poitiers (732)
- Pepin the Short and the new dynasty (751)
- Charlemagne: conquests and the crown of emperor (800)
- The Carolingian renaissance: schools, writing, counts and missi dominici
- The Treaty of Verdun (843): the birth of West Francia
- Vikings and the creation of Normandy (911)
- Feudalism: lords, vassals, fiefs, castles
- Hugh Capet (987): the start of the Capetians

## Period 3: the Capetians build a kingdom (11th to early 14th century)
- The small royal domain and the great princes
- The Church, monasteries and Cluny; the Peace of God
- The crusades (from 1095) and the French
- Towns, trade, fairs of Champagne and communes
- Romanesque and Gothic: Saint-Denis, Notre-Dame, Chartres
- William the Conqueror (1066) and the Plantagenets; Eleanor of Aquitaine
- Philip II Augustus and Bouvines (1214)
- The Albigensian crusade and the South
- Saint Louis (Louis IX): justice, faith, crusades
- Philip IV the Fair: the State, the Templars, the Pope, the first Estates-General
- The end of the direct Capetians and the succession crisis (1328)

## Period 4: crises and the Hundred Years' War (1328 to late 15th century)
- The causes of the Hundred Years' War (1337)
- Crécy (1346) and Calais
- The Black Death (1348)
- John II, Poitiers (1356) and the crisis in Paris (Étienne Marcel)
- Charles V and du Guesclin: the recovery
- Charles VI, Armagnacs and Burgundians
- Agincourt (1415) and the Treaty of Troyes (1420)
- Joan of Arc: Orléans, Reims, Rouen
- The end of the war (1453) and the new royal power (taxes, army)
- Louis XI against Charles the Bold

## Period 5: Renaissance and wars of religion (1494 to 1610)
- The Italian wars and the discovery of Italy
- Francis I: Marignano (1515), the châteaux, Leonardo da Vinci
- The ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts (1539): French as the language of the State
- Humanism and printing; Rabelais, Montaigne
- The Reformation and the French Protestants (Huguenots)
- The wars of religion (from 1562)
- The St Bartholomew's Day massacre (1572)
- Henry IV and the Edict of Nantes (1598)
- France and the Americas: Cartier, Champlain, New France

## Period 6: the absolute monarchy (1610 to 1774)
- Louis XIII and Richelieu
- The Fronde: the nobles and Paris against the young king
- Louis XIV takes power (1661): absolutism
- Versailles: the court as a tool of power
- Colbert: economy, manufactures, navy
- The wars of Louis XIV and the new borders
- Revocation of the Edict of Nantes (1685)
- The colonies, the slave trade and the Code Noir (1685)
- Louis XV: the Regency, Law's system, the end of New France (1763)
- The Enlightenment: Montesquieu, Voltaire, Rousseau, the Encyclopédie
- Society of the Ancien Régime: the three orders

## Period 7: the French Revolution (1774 to 1799)
- Louis XVI and the financial crisis
- The Estates-General (May 1789) and the Tennis Court Oath
- 14 July 1789: the storming of the Bastille
- The night of 4 August and the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen
- The constitutional monarchy, the flight to Varennes (1791)
- War (1792), the fall of the monarchy (10 August), the First Republic
- The trial and execution of Louis XVI (1793)
- The Terror, Robespierre, the Vendée
- The first abolition of slavery (1794) and Saint-Domingue
- Thermidor and the Directory

## Period 8: Napoleon (1799 to 1815)
- Bonaparte, from general to First Consul (18 Brumaire, 1799)
- The Consulate builds modern France: Civil Code, prefects, Concordat, Banque de France
- The Empire (1804) and the great victories (Austerlitz, 1805)
- Slavery restored (1802) and the independence of Haiti (1804)
- The continental blockade, Spain, Russia (1812)
- The fall: 1814, the Hundred Days, Waterloo (1815)

## Period 9: kings, emperor and revolutions (1815 to 1871)
- The Restoration: Louis XVIII and Charles X
- The July Revolution (1830) and the July Monarchy
- The industrial revolution in France: railways, workers, cities
- The 1848 revolution: universal male suffrage, abolition of slavery
- Louis-Napoleon Bonaparte: from president to emperor (1851–1852)
- The Second Empire: Haussmann's Paris, growth, colonial expansion
- The war of 1870 and Sedan
- The Paris Commune (1871)

## Period 10: the Third Republic and the First World War (1870 to 1939)
- The Republic wins (1875–1879): symbols, 14 July, the Marseillaise
- Jules Ferry: free, compulsory and secular school (1881–1882)
- The colonial empire
- The Dreyfus affair
- The 1905 law: separation of Church and State
- The Belle Époque
- The causes of the First World War
- 1914: the Marne; the war of the trenches
- Verdun (1916) and the home front
- 1918, the armistice, the Treaty of Versailles
- The interwar years: crisis, leagues, 6 February 1934
- The Popular Front (1936)

## Period 11: the Second World War (1939 to 1945)
- The phoney war and the defeat of 1940
- Pétain, the Vichy regime and collaboration
- De Gaulle and the Appeal of 18 June
- The Resistance
- The persecution of the Jews; the Vel d'Hiv roundup (1942)
- D-Day and the Liberation (1944)
- Women vote for the first time (1945)

## Period 12: reconstruction and the end of the empire (1945 to 1962)
- The Fourth Republic: Social Security, reconstruction
- The Trente Glorieuses
- Europe begins: ECSC (1951), Treaty of Rome (1957)
- Indochina and Dien Bien Phu (1954)
- The Algerian War (1954–1962)
- 1958: de Gaulle returns, the Fifth Republic

## Period 13: the Fifth Republic (1958 to today)
- The Constitution of 1958 and the president elected by the people (1962)
- De Gaulle's France: independence, nuclear power, Europe
- May 1968
- Pompidou and Giscard d'Estaing: modernisation, the Veil law (1975)
- Mitterrand (1981): alternation, abolition of the death penalty
- Cohabitation, Maastricht (1992), the euro (2002)
- Chirac, Sarkozy, Hollande
- Recent years: present them with care (facts, no opinions)
- France today: what history explains

## Final part
- Great syntheses through the centuries (the State, religion, borders, rights, France and the world)
- Final review: telling the whole story in 20 dates

## After the roadmap
Deepening phase driven by the learner's questions: regions, women in history, arts, everyday life,
historians' debates, and regular reviews every 7th lesson.
